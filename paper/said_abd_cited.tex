\subsection{Sinhala-ABD}
\label{sec:abd-construction}

\paragraph{Source passages and labels.}
Mixed documents are built from contiguous Wikipedia passages of six to twelve
sentences, cut from articles that survive a page-type and prose filter
(Appendix~\ref{app:abd-cleaning}). Every sentence carries a human or machine
label, and boundaries are derived from the label sequence rather than stored
separately, so a boundary is by definition an index where the label changes.
Sentence-level labelling inside a document is a stricter setting than the
document-level judgement of Sinhala-HAT: the detector cannot rely on a global
verdict but must localise a change of authorship within text that is largely
human-written \citep{wang2024semeval,zeng2024aaai}. Releasing labels per
sentence rather than per document also lets the same corpus support both
sentence classification and boundary localisation, which prior hybrid-text
resources treat as separate problems \citep{gao2024llm,zeng2024ijcai}.

\paragraph{Construction types and composition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:abd-constructions} illustrates continuation, single-span
replacement, and multi-span replacement; Table~\ref{tab:abd-composition}
summarises the resulting corpus by construction type and generator.
\emph{Continuation} follows the setting introduced by RoFT
\citep{dugan2022roft} and studied since as a boundary-detection task
\citep{kushnareva2024ai}: a human prefix is followed by machine text, giving a
single boundary. A split point is sampled uniformly between 20\% and 80\% of
the passage; the true remainder is discarded and the model writes a replacement
conditioned on the prefix. The two replacement types instead place machine text
\emph{inside} human text, as in hybrid essays and co-authored documents
\citep{zeng2024aaai,gao2024llm}. In \emph{single-span replacement}, one internal
span of one to three sentences is rewritten, with the model shown the left and
right context but instructed to rewrite only the marked span. In
\emph{multi-span replacement}, two or three non-adjacent spans of one to two
sentences are rewritten, each generated \emph{independently from the original
human passage} rather than sequentially, so the model cannot impose a single
consistent machine register across the document; all spans in a document come
from one generator.

\paragraph{Design constraints.}
Three constraints exist to keep the corpus from being solvable by surface cues
rather than by authorship. Spans may not include the first or last sentence,
and at least one human sentence separates consecutive spans, so replacement
never degenerates into a prefix or suffix change. The 20--80\% sampling and the
first/last-sentence rule together stop position alone from identifying a
boundary; the resulting distribution is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:abd-positions}. Replacing existing text rather than inserting
invented sentences keeps the surrounding passage genuinely human-written and
constrains the machine text to the content it replaces, which prevents topic
from separating the classes \citep{wang2024m4}. As in Sinhala-HAT, generated
spans are length-matched to the text they replace, since unconstrained output
is systematically longer and length alone then becomes a shortcut
\citep{doughman2024limitations}. Documents are never padded into a longer type:
a passage too short for a construction is simply ineligible for it.
Three generators are used, so the corpus is not built around one model's style
\citep{wang2024m4,khairallah2025alhd}, and Gemini~2.5~Pro is generated for a
disjoint subset of source articles so that it can be held out entirely,
supporting the cross-model evaluation that detection results are known to be
sensitive to \citep{macko2023multitude,lucas2026bluff}.
Sinhala-ABD is constructed independently of Sinhala-HAT, although human source
texts may overlap between the two datasets. Sinhala-ABD prompts specify the
task in Sinhala and add English constraint blocks;
Appendix~\ref{app:abd-prompts} reproduces the prompts, including an unused
reverse-continuation family.

\paragraph{Automatic validation of Sinhala-ABD.}
Because a mixed document's labels are only correct if the generated span is
actually usable in place of the text it replaced, every generation passes seven
automatic checks before it enters the corpus: exact sentence count; word count
within $\pm$25\% of the replaced span; no preamble; no Markdown; predominantly
Sinhala script; not a near-copy of the original span under normalised
comparison; and preservation of numeric tokens, so a date or quantity cannot
silently mutate. A failing generation is retried up to three times and then
discarded, and outputs are never edited by hand, so the corpus contains only
text the generators actually produced. Across 5{,}109 rejected generations,
which record 5{,}951 individual check failures since one generation may fail
several checks at once, 53.7\% of failures were length violations, 23.9\%
sentence-count violations, 16.3\% number mutations, 3.5\% script violations,
2.3\% near-copies, and 0.3\% formatting. The length tolerance was deliberately
not relaxed: all three generators under-write Sinhala relative to the human
span, and admitting shorter machine spans would let sentence length substitute
for authorship as a detection signal \citep{doughman2024limitations}. Removing
formatting and preamble symmetrically, rather than normalising the text
further, follows the same reasoning as for Sinhala-HAT: punctuation and surface
form themselves carry authorship information, so aggressive cleaning would
destroy signal and introduce class-specific artifacts
\citep{mcgovern2024fingerprints}. 76.8\% of accepted documents passed on the
first attempt, 15.2\% after one retry, and 8.0\% after two or more.
